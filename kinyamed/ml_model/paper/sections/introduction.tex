\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A nurse facing a full waiting room has to decide who is seen first. Triage
systems support that decision, and the ones in general use --- the Emergency
Severity Index, the Manchester Triage System --- assume a shared clinical
language between patient and assessor. In a Rwandan health centre the patient
describes their illness in Kinyarwanda, and almost every tool that could read
that description was built for English.

This paper reports a dataset and a classifier for three-level urgency triage
(critical, urgent, routine) from patient-voice Kinyarwanda. Our contribution is
less the classifier than the account of what it took to make one honestly, and
the two failures we found while doing so.

\paragraph{The language problem is not a translation problem.} Our first corpus
was generated by a non-speaker from English clinical concepts and machine
translation, and it was not usable. A Kinyarwanda speaker reading it found
grammatical sentences that no patient would say: English syntax carried across
intact, register drifted toward the clinical, and borrowings that real speech
uses freely avoided in favour of ``purer'' constructions. The speaker also
rejected the grammatical \emph{form} the generator required. That is not a
quality gap a larger model closes. It is the difference between text about a
language and text in it, and the only remedy we found was to have the phrases
authored rather than translated.

\paragraph{Small corpora make evaluation harder, not easier.} A corpus assembled
from a few hundred authored sentences and combinatorially expanded looks large
and is not. Our evaluation set contains 17{,}942 rows and nine distinct
sentences, and per-class recall therefore moves in steps of about a quarter.
Every quantitative claim in this paper is bounded by that, and we state the
distinct-sentence count beside every recall figure rather than the row count.

\paragraph{Two failures worth more than the model.} We report both because both
were recorded correctly and acted on late.

The first is an evaluation split that never worked. Its manifest recorded that
100\% of its evaluation rows were built on phrases also present in training ---
in a machine-readable field, in the commit that created the pipeline. The two
fields anyone read were both honestly zero. The measurement was not missing; the
reading was.

The second is our acceptance gate. It was a single condition --- recall on the
critical class --- chosen for the defensible reason that missing a critical
presentation is the failure that matters. A model passed it while assigning the
critical label to almost every urgent case, and its precision on that class sat
\emph{below} the score that merging the two classes earns for free on the same
rows. The gate certified the worse of two models, and had no term in which
over-triage could be expressed.

\paragraph{Scope, stated plainly.} The corpus is Kinyarwanda only. Roughly half
its phrases are authored directly by one speaker and the remainder are machine
drafts under that speaker's approval, recorded per phrase. No second speaker has
reviewed it and no clinician has signed off on it. The model we report does not
meet our own acceptance gate. Nothing here is in use with any patient, and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} is the section we would most want a reader to
weigh.

\paragraph{We do not claim priority.} We are not aware of a prior patient-voice
medical triage dataset for Kinyarwanda, and the resource audit described in
Section~\ref{sec:related} did not locate one. We have not run a systematic
search of the field, and we make no stronger claim than that.
