\begin{abstract}
Triage decides who is seen first. Building an urgency classifier for
patient-voice Kinyarwanda, we found that the specification we were building to
could be satisfied without producing anything it was meant to secure. We report
that, and the instruments that detect it, instead of a classifier.

Our corpus requirement asked for one million examples. Generation produced them
in 130 seconds. Run against the nine corpus-quality gates written in the same
specification, that corpus fails four as generated and six where its rows can be
attributed to a source sentence. The binding gate counts distinct authored seed
phrases, not rows, and the generator has 165 of them: at that inventory the
largest corpus passing every gate is zero rows, at any row count. Two distinct
shortfalls follow, and they are not the same number. Passing the seed-count gate
needs 2{,}835 further authored sentences (a floor of 3{,}000). Reaching the
stated one million rows needs 19{,}835 further sentences, because a separate
gate caps a seed at 50 rows and 1{,}000{,}000 rows therefore require 20{,}000
seeds. Rows are produced by machine at roughly 7{,}700 per second; seeds are
written by native-speaking clinicians. We argue the general form: a corpus requirement stated as a row count
constrains the cheap quantity and leaves the expensive one free, so it does not
constrain quality at all.

Two further negatives follow from the same root. First, an evaluation set of
17{,}942 rows built from nine distinct source sentences supports no verdict: our
deployment gate, which counts distinct sentences rather than rows, refuses 38 of
its cells and declines to report. Second, a model trained on a corpus with
uniform surface form changes its predicted urgency under surface variation alone:
31.5\% of inputs under capitalisation and 21.0\% under a single typo, while
whitespace, which the tokenizer normalises, changes nothing.

We also report a deployment failure mode that leaves every output-side signal
healthy: a model directory shipped without its tokenizer loads without error,
returns a five-token vocabulary, and answers its class prior on input it cannot
read, with well-formed probabilities and deterministic predictions. We mistook
it for single-class collapse in our own harness.

No figure in this paper is offered as evidence of model quality. Some model
numbers appear, because the failures we report are about them, but none is a
performance claim: no evaluation set meeting our own specification exists, our
gate refuses on the set that does, and the trained artefact is an audit artefact
rather than a baseline. The
contribution is the measurement apparatus and the negative results it produced,
each reproducible from a clean clone with one command.
\end{abstract}
