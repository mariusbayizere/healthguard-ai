# Vocabulary expansion: where the corpus is thin and what it would take

The corpus is 1,000,000 rows expanded from **184 seed phrases**. That number, not
the row count, is the ceiling on everything the dataset can demonstrate.

## Current coverage

46 phrases per language, identical structure across all four languages.

```
  domain                  kinyarwanda  english   french  swahili   TOTAL
  obstetric                         2        2        2        2       8
  chronic_care                      4        4        4        4      16
  paediatric                        4        4        4        4      16
  preventive                        4        4        4        4      16
  gastrointestinal                  6        6        6        6      24
  haemorrhage_trauma                6        6        6        6      24
  neurological                      6        6        6        6      24
  cardiac_respiratory               7        7        7        7      28
  infectious_fever                  7        7        7        7      28
  TOTAL                            46       46       46       46     184

  by urgency: CRITICAL 68 | URGENT 68 | ROUTINE 48
```

## Where it is thinnest

**Obstetric, at 2 phrases per language, is the weakest point** — and the one that
matters most. It is the smallest domain in the corpus (38,820 rows) and it carries
the presentations where under-triage is least recoverable: post-partum
haemorrhage, eclampsia, obstructed labour. Two phrasings per language cannot
represent that.

Next: `chronic_care`, `paediatric` and `preventive` at 4 each.

The deeper thinness is in the eval, not the corpus. The phrase holdout removes
**16 of 184 phrases**, so every claim about generalising to unseen wording rests
on 16 phrasings spread across 5 languages and 3 classes. Several language x class
cells in the eval matrix are backed by one or two distinct phrasings.

## What target changes the paper's claim

The Limitations paragraph currently concedes "on the order of 184 clinically
distinct items, not a million". Two useful thresholds:

| target | per language | total | held out at 10% | what it buys |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1** — every domain to 8/language | 72 | **288** | ~29 | obstetric stops being a stub; the claim becomes "~290 items" |
| **Phase 2** — every domain to 14/language | 126 | **504** | ~50 | median rows/phrase falls 5,674 -> ~2,000; the holdout rests on ~50 phrasings instead of 16 |

Phase 2 is the point at which the phrase-holdout number starts to mean something
per language x class cell rather than only in aggregate.

## Phrases cannot be added without refreezing — verified

Adding a single phrase (184 -> 185) and regenerating with the same seed 42:

    before (= committed manifest) : e75057b5e73070d5ef2fa9ea76dca140be40e9e1
    after  (one phrase added)     : 17eae4035c64c771f241a631e7179353ac9a546c

The corpus digest changes completely, so `eval_manifest_phrase_v1.json` and
`eval_manifest_family_v1.json` both become invalid and must be re-frozen as **v2**.

**Sequencing consequence.** Any training result produced against v1 is not
comparable to any result produced against v2. Running 20 epochs on v1 and then
expanding the vocabulary means the run's numbers are historical — they describe a
corpus the paper will no longer be about. If the vocabulary is going to grow, it
is cheaper to grow it first and train once.

## What I can draft, and what I cannot

**I can draft, for review:**

- English and French candidate phrasings, including the clinical shape of each
  domain's missing presentations (obstetric especially).
- The structural work: extending `vocabulary.py`, keeping per-language and
  per-class balance, regenerating, re-validating, re-freezing to v2, and updating
  every count in the README and the paper so they stay accurate.
- A gap list: which domain x language x urgency cells are underweight and how many
  phrases each needs to reach a target.

**I cannot validate, and should not be trusted on:**

- **Kinyarwanda and Swahili medical register.** I can produce candidate strings,
  but not whether they are what a patient would actually say, whether the register
  is right, or whether a phrase reads as clinical rather than as literal
  translation. These need a native speaker. Treat anything I generate in those
  languages as an unverified draft, never as corpus-ready.
- **Code-switching realism.** "mixed" is 48% of the corpus but is generated by
  combining monolingual phrases. Whether those combinations resemble how bilingual
  Rwandans actually speak is a question about real speech, and I have no basis for
  it.
- **Urgency labels.** Whether a presentation is CRITICAL rather than URGENT is a
  clinical judgement. The current labels are internally consistent because the
  template assigns them, not because anyone qualified reviewed them. Every new
  phrase needs a clinician to set its class, and the existing 184 deserve the same
  review.

The honest division: I do the scaffolding and the English/French drafting; a
Kinyarwanda speaker and a clinician own the two things that make the corpus mean
anything.
