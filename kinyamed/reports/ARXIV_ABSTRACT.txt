We set out to build an urgency classifier for patient-voice Kinyarwanda, and report
what we found instead.

Our corpus requirement asked for one million examples. Generation produced them in
130 seconds. Against the nine corpus-quality gates in the same specification, that
corpus fails four as generated, and six where its rows can be attributed to a source
sentence. The binding gate counts distinct authored seed phrases rather than rows,
and our generator has 165: at that inventory the largest corpus passing every gate
is zero rows, at any row count. The distance to the target is 19,835 authored
sentences, not 999,000 rows. Expansion is the cheap operation in any templated or
model-expanded corpus, so a requirement written in rows leaves unconstrained the one
quantity that bounds what a model can learn.

Two further negatives follow. An evaluation set of 17,942 rows built from nine
distinct sentences supports no verdict: our gate, which counts distinct sentences
and resamples them for every interval, refuses 38 of its cells and reports nothing.
A model trained on a corpus of uniform surface form changes its predicted urgency
under surface variation alone -- 31.5% of inputs under capitalisation, 21.0% under
one typo -- while extra whitespace, which the tokenizer normalises, changes nothing.

We also report a failure mode that leaves every output-side signal healthy: a model
shipped without its tokenizer loads without error, returns a five-token vocabulary,
and answers its class prior on unreadable input, with well-formed probabilities and
deterministic predictions.

We report no accuracy or calibration figure: no evaluation set meeting our
specification exists, and the trained artefact is an audit artefact, not a baseline.
The contribution is the apparatus and the negative results it produced, each
reproducible from a clean clone with one command.
